% Abstract (Do not insert blank lines, i.e. \\) 
\abstract{In this paper, we describe the two related scripting methods of cartographic data processing and visualization that provide 2D and 3D mapping of Japan with different algorithm complexity. The first algorithm utilizes Generic Mapping Toolset (GMT) known as an advanced console-based program for spatial data processing. The modules of GMT combine the functionality of scripting with the aspects of geoinformatics which is especially effective for rapid analysis of large geospatial datasets, multi-format data processing and mapping in 2D and 3D modes. The second algorithm presents the use of the R programming language for cartographic visualization and spatial analysis. This R method utilizes the packages ‘tmap’, ‘raster’, ‘maps’, ‘mapdata’ to model the geomorphometric elements of the Japanese archipelago, such as slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation. The general purpose graphical package ‘ggplot2’ of R was used for mapping prefectures of Japan. The two scripting approaches demonstrated an established correspondence between the programming languages and cartography, which is determined with the use of scripts for data processing. They outperform several well-known and state-of-the-art GIS methods for mapping due to high automation of data processing. Cartography has largely reflected recent advances in data science, rapid development of scripting languages and transfer in the approaches of data processing. This extends to the shift from the traditional GIS to programming languages. As a response to these new challenges, we demonstrated in this paper the advantages of using scripts in mapping, which consist in repeatability and flexible applicability of scripts in similar works. The graphical plotting in 2D and 3D modes is performed in RStudio and GMT.}

\section{Introduction}

Cartographic visualization is an important component of many Earth science applications. In numeric land modelling, the discriminative power of 2D and 3D mapping is a key factor in the topographic analysis of land features. During the last four decades a variety of Geographic Information Systems (GIS) have been developed, with commercial ArcGIS software certainly being the most widely used. Land surface modelling by GIS aims at qualitative data analysis, cartographic visualization and interpretation of the topographic features visualized on maps to report findings on spatial heterogeneity and variability of the objects and environmental processes on the Earth. The functionality of various GIS is in many cases tailored to the specific tasks. For instance, some are better suited for image processing, such as Erdas Imagine \cite{IWAHASHI2010202,AHN2010284}, Idrisi GIS \cite{land10040379,AYALEW200515}, ILWIS GIS \cite{HENGL2009309,BABIKER2005127}, SAGA GIS \cite{CHALUPA2018174,Lemenkova2021a}, ENVI GIS, while others are best at vector data analysis and visualization, e.g., ArcGIS \cite{OHTA2022108201,IKEMI2017115,IWAHASHI201229,Klauco2013,NAKAYAMA2023110243,HANAOKA2014274}.
Most of these GIS are based on standard interface with a restricted functionality that requires manual processing of data, although recently machine learning techniques have been applied to spatial data modelling \cite{THONGTHAMMACHART2022105447,MAO2022113082} and image analysis \cite{ALIFU2020107365,CARRASCO2022277}. However, although these methods succeed in improving the cartographic workflow over the performance of advanced algorithms, they use the pre-defined algorithms in GIS and this can result in the restricted cartographic functionality of programs for data being modelled.

Topographic and geomorphological mapping, however, should aim at all research stages, at the machine-learning approach, which is less error-prone and time-consuming, comparing to the traditional GIS. At the very least, this can be the use of scripting for data visualization, as in Generic Mapping Tools (GMT) \cite{FARAG2022229297,data7060074}. Despite certain difficulties in mastering the program \cite{FANG202031}, such as high learning curve, console-based operation mode and complex steps of fine-tuning map elements, GMT nevertheless provides a much more powerful functionality of cartographic workflow and flexibility of data processing. 

Better still, however, is using a full functionality of the programming and machine learning \cite{OUATTARA202015777,LEMENKOVA_2019}, application of scripting languages for plugins and auxiliary tools \cite{NAGHIBI2021101232,GUO2022105354,Lemenkov2021,SEBBAH2021104972,CHEN2022105362}. Spatial analysis can benefit from the automation of the cartographic processes because scripting enables repeatability of the process. The advanced modelling enables to perform a more comprehensive analysis of various factors affecting land surface and processes \cite{KAPPES2012139}. 

In this paper, we propose a scripting framework that, by facilitating mapping process using powerful methods of programming, can map 2D and 3D maps more effectively than previous techniques of GIS. In turn, accurate visualization of complex terrain models can lead to an increased precision and effectiveness of maps. Cartographic layout is usually seen as the final stage of spatial analysis summarising and presenting the results in a graphical form. That said, scripting by R land GMT largely facilitates graphic visualization, since similar parts of scripts may be re-used for other maps. The contribution of the paper is twofold. First, the emphasis towards creating a script-based data visualization offers the increased precision of the geomorphological analysis through automation of data processing. Second, it provides an important advances in development of cartographic methods, since using R and GMT in cartographic workflow paved the way for the machine learning methods in geospatial data analysis and cartography.

\subsection{Background and Motivation}
Topography is an important characteristics of the land surface, which is reflected in the landscape and associated with numerous environmental factors. Specifically, the geomorphic features control and affect vegetation coverage \cite{land10060559,GOMEZ201511}, habitats and landforms \cite{land7030102,land10101031}, hydrology, soil distribution, and local micro-climate setting that are dependent on the topographic exposure, slope steepness and curvature of the relief. 

As a result, spatial characteristics that can be retrieved from the topographic and land surface maps have many applications, such as environmental management  \cite{land6040083}, flood monitoring \cite{HOOKE2020106793}, hydrological and fluvial modelling in riverine ecosystems \cite{SIAKEU2004231,NAKAYAMA201362}, analysis of coastal processes \cite{ITO2022102527}, soil management practices and crop yields \cite{TABUCHI20174,PRIYA2001113,SASAI2012175}. Thus, land surface maps present an information background for the analysis of correlation between the geomorphology and geology \cite{MATSUURA20141}, geophysical and geographic factors \cite{IMAIZUMI2018125}. 

Topographic mapping based on Digital Elevation Model (DEM) is considered one of the most important issues of cartography often used as a background information for spatial analysis in geosciences. An inherent research step in the computations of the terrain analysis based on DEM is the use of modelling methods where GIS is traditionally applied. A variety of existing GIS software can be used for spatial data visualization and topographic analysis considered as a background for both socio-geographic and physical-geographic mapping. Since the onset of the GIS development, technical methods of cartographic visualization, approaches of terrain analysis are constantly improved \cite{Lam,HUTCHINSON1989211,Deng}. 

\subsection{Related Work}

Various publications focused on Japan were published in particular, aimed to understand how the geomorphology of the in the Japanese Alps is linked to the the geologic and surface structure \cite{MATSUOKA201965}, rock glacial processes \cite{Ono1980} and distribution of vegetation \cite{FUJINO2023100146}, as well as reflected in the adjacent bathymetry \cite{1974607,1980319,Kariya2002}. Multivariate methods of spatial analysis that can be used for deeper understanding of land surface processes include a variety of analytic techniques aimed at quantitative analysis of geospatial data using  coordinates which locate them \cite{Chorley}. These can be used as the advanced tools enabling to perform a deeper analysis on the highly heterogeneous geomorphological setting of Japan, as reflected in recent relevant works \cite{Oguchi2000,OGUCHI2020106805,MATSUURA2015133,NIWA2021107835,HATTANJI2021107943}. 

Providing a standardised solution of selecting proper GIS is a challenge since in most cases it has a similar functionality of both raster and vector data analysis. However, whichever GIS is chosen, it requires active work in an interface menu with manipulation of input data and modelling to present cartographic visualization. Another point is the issue of availability which has a restricted access for the commercial GIS. The increasing variety of GIS necessitates that alternative more flexible and varied approaches to cartographic visualization and geospatial modelling should be explored. Such alternatives are presented by scripting approaches which can provide another functional tool for cartographic visualization and geospatial modelling. Examples of scripting toolsets in cartography include GRASS GIS \cite{Lemenkova202016,Lemenkova202026} and GMT used in topographic and geomorphic mapping \cite{ijgi11090473,geosciences12030140}. 

\subsection{Contribution}

This paper presents the application of GMT cartographic scripting toolset \cite{Wesseletal2019} and R language \cite{RCoreTeam} for spatial analysis and mapping of terrain in Japan. We demonstrated the main advantages of both scripting approaches which consist in their straightforward and logical language syntax, a scripting approach and open source access. We used a powerful functionality of GMT modules for fine-tuning of the cartographic visualization, and R packages to process both tabular and geospatial data directly from a console. We considered the existing experience of applications of R libraries in previous studies on geospatial data processing and visualisation. For instance, special packages for geographic data processing include gstat \cite{HENGL20081886}, RStoolbox \cite{app122412554}, terra or raster \cite{jimaging8120317}.

This study makes a technical contribution to a development by adapting R language for mapping instead of the traditional GIS. This paper shown application of GMT scripting toolset and R language for cartographic data visualization and geomorphometric analysis which included following general steps for mapping Japan: 1) GEBCO, ETOPO1 and ETOPO2 data processing by GMT for 2D and 3D mapping by scripts; 2) SRTM90 DEM importing by R; 2) visualising a DEM using 'raster' package; 3) computing topographic surface parameters of slope, aspect and hillshade using R scripts; 4) cartographic visualization by 'tmap' package of R with the additional cartographic elements (legend, histogram, grid, ticks, annotations, compass directions and a bar scale). 

The structure of this document is as follows. First, we discussed the key issues relevant to the geomorphometric modelling based on DEM in cartographic analysis of the geospatial data. Specifically, slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation are reviewed as important elements of the terrain analysis. Second, we presented the study area of the Japanese archipelago as a case example for mapping and briefly outlined major geographic features of the Japanese Alps. Third, we presented the technical noted on scripts followed by a methodological description of the performed geomorphometric analysis using R and GMT. The full scripts are listed in the Appendix as a technical reference for similar studies. Besides, the methodological part demonstrates the screenshots of scripting in RStudio environment where maps of slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation have been plotted, and GMT for 2D and 3D modelling. 

The results present the computed and visualized maps of the Japanese archipelago made by GMT scripting toolset and in the RStudio. The latter ones include the geomorphometric elements (slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation) with statistical elements of data distribution as histograms, and a map of prefectures of Japan visualized by the ‘ggplot2’ graphical package of R. The discussion of the machine based approach to cartographic modelling and visualization follows the results section. Finally, we provided the conclusions regarding the advantages of using GMT and R in cartography instead of state-of-the-art GIS techniques based on standard graphical user's interface, and ways of modelling geospatial data by scripting languages from the console.

\section{Study Area}

The geomorphology of Japan is characterised by the early stage of mountain range formation comprising young and active island arcs \cite{SUEOKA2022229231}. As a results of the complex tectonic movements and the uplift and denudation processes in the Japanese Islands, the Japanese Alps were formed as a chain of mountain ranges that roughly divide the main island of Honshu. During Quaternary, the major axis of the Japan Alps experienced more than 2000 m of uplift which resulted in the exposure of the Takidani Granodiorite \cite{Harayama}.  

\begin{figure}[H]
	\includegraphics[width=14.0 cm]{Fig-01}
\caption{Topographic map of Japan. Data source: GEBCO. Software: GMT, version 6.1.1. Cartography source: authors. \label{fig01}}

The regions is divided into three major parts: Northern, Central and Southern Alps. The northern region located in the Nagano, Toyama and Gifu prefectures includes Hida Mountains with high seismicity along one of major Quaternary faults -- the Atotsugawa fault with repetitive earthquakes \cite{MIKUMO198895,ENESCU2005147}. The geologic structure of Hida terrane is presented by the complex geologic structure with gneisses formed from Permo-Carboniferous clastic sediments during a single metamorphism at ca. 250 Ma \cite{SUZUKI1994277,TSUJIMORI2006167}. Northern Japanese Alps are characterised by  lateral and terminal moraines and outwash terraces of glacial origin. The Central Alps include the Kiso Mountains located in the Nagano prefecture \cite{SUEOKA2016197} and characterised by granite structure. The highest peak of the Japanese Alps is recorded in Mt. Kita (3,193 m) in the Southern Alps, or Akaishi Mountains.

The Japanese Alps had long been exploited with described geologic structure and geomorphic landforms \cite{KARIYA2011156} and tectonic evolution \cite{burbank2009tectonic,Sato}. Besides the environmental value and strong effects on the climate and vegetation setting of Japan, the Japanese Alps contribute to the economy of the country, being the source of natural mineral resources such as timber and minerals, as well as habitat for diverse species and vegetation, including medical herbs. Moreover, rice paddy fields are cultivated on the slopes of the mountains. Finally, the Alps are considered as a potential source of geothermal energy \cite{TSUCHIYA2017193}. All these factors make the Japanese Alps a key land surface object for nature and society of Japan.

\section{Materials and Methods}
The key methods include the GMT with diverse modules and packages of Rm including ‘tmap’ for thematic mapping, ‘ggplot2’, ‘ggmap’ \cite{KahleWickham}, ‘maps’ and ‘mapdata’ for data capture and thematic regional mapping of prefectures of Japan. The package ‘mapdata’ contains the base location of the binary files of prefectures boundaries of Japan used by the map drawing functions. The ‘raster’ package was used for geomorphometric analysis which also included the depending packages, such as ‘sp’, a package which operates with classes and methods for spatial data, and ‘sf’, a package operating with ‘Simple Features’ as object classes in an R syntax.

The data for the terrain analysis includes digital elevation models (DEM) from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM). The SRTM is widely used in research \cite{RABUS2003241} due to its acceptable resolution (90m*90m) and open source availability: the NASA Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) provided DEMs for over 80\% of the land surface on the Earth \cite{Ehsani}. The slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation maps were plotted using RStudio environment \cite{RStudio} using ‘raster’ package implemented by \cite{Hijmans}. Specifically the following workflow was used as a data pre-processing. The data were captured using the ‘getData()’ function of the ‘raster’ package of R from the available geospatial datasets of the University of California, Davis campus. 

The data were then reprojected to the Lambert Conformal Conic projection (LCC) by the following function: $crs(alt) <- "+proj=lcc +lat\_1=-30 +lat\_2=40 +lon\_0=140 +datum=WGS84".$ The coordinate system is an important aspect of geographic data which is implemented in R environment by PROJ library \cite{EversKnudsen,Evenden1995,Evenden2005}.   It should be specially mentioned that the SRTM shows elevation data not as a bare-earth model but as a surface which includes dense canopy forests and built-up areas in the estimation of the terrain. Although such nuances might be worrying, in hydrological modelling, the SRTM is generally acceptable in country-level mapping, as in our case for mapping Japan, based on DEM.

\subsection{Mapping the prefectures of Japan}

To illustrate the location of various prefectures of Japan, we used the "ggplot2" packages of R for plotting the regions of prefectures using arguments in the dataset for visualization. The data were captured by the ‘maps’ package of R \cite{Beckeretal2013} and ‘mapdata’ which provide the map databases including the data on Japan: its prefectures, areas, etc. The advantage of this approach consists in integrated using of the ‘mapdata’ with the ‘ggplot2’ package of R, which is a common graphical package designed for general purpose scientific visualization, and applicable for cartographic purposes as well. Here the main elements, i.e., the polygons of the prefectures in Japan, were plotted using the $‘geom\_polygon’$ function, which operates with data frames containing the coordinates of polygons and values associated with each of them: 
\begin{verbatim}geom_polygon(data=japan, aes(x=long, y=lat, fill=region, group=group)\end{verbatim} 

The annotations of the axes were added using ‘xlab’ and ‘ylab’ functions. The color scales were defined in the RColorBrewer, and extended from the default, fixed number of colors to the number of prefectures in Japan which was inspected by the ‘length’ function as follows: 
\begin{verbatim}
	length(unique(japan$region))
\end{verbatim} 
Afterwards, the number of colors in the color palette was defined using the ‘colourCount’ function: 
\begin{verbatim}
	colourCount = length(unique(japan$region))
\end{verbatim}

Following that, the color palette was expanded to the required number (47 prefectures of Japan) by the use of ‘colorRampPalette’ function as follows: $colorRampPalette(brewer.pal(name="Spectral", n = 8))(47)$. 
Now when the color palette was adjusted to the dataset, the map of prefectures of Japan was colored by assigning each individual color to each of the 47 prefectures, respectively, as follows: $scale\_fill\_manual(values = getPalette(colourCount))$. Other, additional cartographic elements included adding the tittles, subtitles and captions by the ‘labs’ function which enables to modify the labels, annotations and captions. The rest of the aesthetics has been added using $theme()$ function as can be seen in Figure \ref{fig3} (left: script, right: resulting map). These include, among others, plotting the legend, its orientation and annotations, defining the text size and font characteristics, selecting the color of background, choosing the types of grid ticks, frequency of breaks on a cartographic grid. The final map is shown in Figure \ref{fig3}.

\subsection{Mapping geomorphological features}
Modeling slope, aspect and hillshade is a technique for visualising terrain determined by DEM as a numerical data source. Here the slope and the aspect have been plotted first as major features. Following that we visualised the hillshade which is a derivative from the slope and aspect of the elevation. The parameters of hillshade were adjusted and visually changed using various illuminating position of the light source as degree of simulated sun angle rotation. The four derivative of the SRTM90 DEM grid were modelled and visualized using R as follows: slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation. 

The slope gradient, aspect and hillshade were defined by the algorithm of the ‘raster’ package at any point of the input raster grid (SRTM90) using local neighbourhood analysis. These variables are automatically defined by the machine using values of altitude (elevation) and its derivatives at or around cell point on a raster grid representing an Earth land surface. The ‘alt’ has a formal class 'RasterLayer' of package ‘raster’ with 12 slots.

\paragraph{Slope}
The terrain characteristics of slope has been calculated and modeled using ‘raster’ package of R using the following sequence of commands. First, the color palette of R was created for visualization: $cols <- rainbow(255)$. Second, the slope and aspect have been computed by following: $slope = terrain(alt, opt = "slope")$. Third, the slope was visualised: $plot(slope, col=cols, main='Slope', xlab = "lon", ylab = "lat")$. Here the ‘alt’ represents the abbreviation of ‘altitude’.

\paragraph{Aspect}
The aspect has been modelled using the function ‘terrain’ with the option ‘aspect’ as follows: $aspect = terrain(alt, opt = "aspect")$, followed by the definition of colors: $cols <- terrain.colors(255)$. Afterwards, using the same function $plot()$ as for the slope modeling, the map of aspect has been plotted using the following snippet of code: $plot(aspect, col=cols, main='Exposure', xlab = "lon", ylab = "lat")$. The full script of R is provided in the Appendix.
 
 \paragraph{Hillshade} 
The topographic hillshade represents the remarkable phenomenon in cartography that occurs when shadow caused by elevation cue for the three-dimensional shape perception \cite{Biland,imhof2015cartographic}. Various techniques exist to produce a monochrome 3D view of the terrain relief with the relative position of the artificial illumination representing the shadows from natural sun \cite{Judd,9222263}. The effects from shading are well reflected in a highly rugged terrain where high elevation land surface uses strong luminance contrast, while low heights with low contrast, as noted prevoiusly \cite{Jenny}. It the attempts to visualise the relief using shading effects of the monochrome palette, the efforts have been made since the onset of the GIS development and considered in this work \cite{1671906,Kimerling}. The computing of hillshade has been done using the following algorithm: $hill = hillShade(slope, aspect, angle = 40, direction = 270)$. The color palette has been applied using the code snippet: $cols <- topo.colors(255)$. Plotting the map has been done using plot function as described above.
 
 \paragraph{Cartographic processing}
Mapping the geomorphological parameters in ‘tmap’ package \cite{JSSv084i06} included the processing of the computed raster layers by a specifically designed cartographic package where more elements could be added on the layout and more control on their aesthetics is available. The package is using a functions $tmap\_style$ where general style of the layout was defined. The $tm\_shape$ was used to define the main data: name of the raster, title and subtitle. Likewise, the $tm\_raster$ function was used to process the color scale of the numeric variables (values of slopes in degrees), labels, and other cartographic details on legend.

The $tm\_scale\_bar$ and $tm\_compass$ functions show the parameters of the annotations of the auxiliary elements on the map. The $tm\_layout$ function shows the variety of the cartographic aesthetics necessary for proper visualization, such as fonts and ticks or color of panel labels. \section{Discussion}

Using the new ways of measuring, sensing, and analysing mountain geomorphology, It was noted \cite{SLAYMAKER2018230} that the range of the mountainous relief is reinforced by the high elevation landforms, as well as external factors, such as and hydrological and climate processes contributing to the erosion and denudation. This is supported by the findings of \cite{Aoyama2005} who noted existing strong correlation between the geomorphology and the environmental settings in Japan. This is proved by the reported findings that geomorphological and climate conditions during the Late Glacial resulted in the change in the environmental setting in the northern Japanese Alps, with shift from  glacial to the permafrost environment in the current alpine zone. 
 
Other important findings in geomorphic studies in Japan are well summarized by \cite{OGUCHI20013} who noted extensive sedimentation in mountain piedmonts and coastal fluvial plains and abundant sediment in steep watersheds. They furthermore pointed at risk of hazardous hydro-geomorphological events in the areas of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions, as well as post glacial development of hill slopes and alluvial fans strongly affected by paleoclimate conditions. Besides the environmental factors, relief directly influences the social-economic patterns through the possibility of roads and buildings constructions and potential hazards of landslides which depend on the rock properties and slope steepness. 

 % checked by 25.12.2022
The geomorphic processes are largely driven by Earth's gravity, which is partially controlled by the slope steepness of the relief. As a result, the intensity of the geomorphic processes, such as landslides,  is affected by displacement gradients connected to the geometric curvature of the surface. Thus, quantified slope steepness enables to calculate the rates at which landslides are stripping mountain slopes, which can be used for risk management and estimation of hazard risk caused by landslides \cite{FUJISAWA2010150,Chung}, in addition to climatic factors, such as precipitation intensity and repeatability of rainfall or downpours \cite{Tsunetaka,Oku,HUANG2022108236} or seismic triggers, such as earthquakes or typhoons \cite{Yamada,Wangetal2010,HIRATA2019105217}. 

As a consequence, there is abundant research into topographic and bathymetric mapping, land surface classification based on DEM, relief and geomorphological modelling using various GIS, geomorphometric computations of slope and aspect, visualizing hillshade and DEM \cite{PRIMA2006373,LIN2004159,FisherTate,HAYAKAWA200690}. Since the geomorphometric studies should always be accompanies by a spatial topographic representation, the question of technical tools of mapping is always actual for the DEM based studies. This research demonstrated that the use of R in geomorphometric studies is effective and promising both for thematic maps plotting and for the spatial analysis.

\section{Conclusions}

Graphics and maps, done well, can provide an eye-catching and detailed information on the Earth’s surface. That is why the graphical approaches and methodology of visualization are of high importance in geographical sciences. Although having a specific language syntax and approaches to data analysis, R language is a very promising tool for geospatial visualization, geomorphological modelling and mapping. Besides the statistical analysis of R applied in various research \cite{GRUNSKY20021219,Lemenkova201991,BIVAND20001043}, it can also be effectively used in mapping, as demonstrated in this study. 

Cartographic data visualization, whatever GIS software are used, is an integral part of the complex chain of the geospatial analysis that includes data capture, preprocessing, projecting, analysis of content of the datasets, modelling variables and soon. Applying machine learning methods to cartographic workflow facilitates the process of spatial analysis, helps increasing the accuracy and precision of the final results, and significantly decreases the time of data processing. The use of R in geographic analysis, however, is not yet as popular as traditional GIS, which can be explained by the non-trivial approach of using it in mapping and certain skills in coding.  

In conclusion, the findings indicate that using R for mapping is another effective tool for spatial analysis besides GIS. It furthermore identified the issues, such as the need to use a variety of packages in R to fit in various tasks: geomorphometry, classic mapping, spatial analysis, data capture, data conversion are all were made using different packages. On the one hand it can be an easier than in GIS, but on the other hand, it can be a challenge since it require finding a suitable package, installing and activating it and using by applying its specific functions using syntax of R.

# Aspect

cols <- terrain.colors(255)
