% Abstract (Do not insert blank lines, i.e. \\) 
\abstract{In this paper, we describe the two related scripting methods of cartographic data processing and visualization that provide 2D and 3D mapping of Japan with different algorithm complexity. The first algorithm utilizes Generic Mapping Toolset (GMT) known as an advanced console-based program for spatial data processing. The modules of GMT combine the functionality of scripting with the aspects of geoinformatics which is especially effective for rapid analysis of large geospatial datasets, multi-format data processing and mapping in 2D and 3D modes. The second algorithm presents the use of the R programming language for cartographic visualization and spatial analysis. This R method utilizes the packages ‘tmap’, ‘raster’, ‘maps’, ‘mapdata’ to model the geomorphometric elements of the Japanese archipelago, such as slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation. The general purpose graphical package ‘ggplot2’ of R was used for mapping prefectures of Japan. The two scripting approaches demonstrated an established correspondence between the programming languages and cartography, which is determined with the use of scripts for data processing. They outperform several well-known and state-of-the-art GIS methods for mapping due to high automation of data processing. Cartography has largely reflected recent advances in data science, rapid development of scripting languages and transfer in the approaches of data processing. This extends to the shift from the traditional GIS to programming languages. As a response to these new challenges, we demonstrated in this paper the advantages of using scripts in mapping, which consist in repeatability and flexible applicability of scripts in similar works. The graphical plotting in 2D and 3D modes is performed in RStudio and GMT.}

\section{Introduction}

Topographic analysis based on DEM is considered one of the most important issues of cartography often used as a background information in geosciences. An inherent research step in the computations of the terrain analysis based on DEM is the use of modelling methods where the GIS is traditionally applied. A variety of existing GIS software can be applied to spatial data analysis which includes both socio-geographic and physical-geographic mapping. Modelling geospatial datasets by GIS aims at qualitative data analysis, cartographic visualization and interpretation of the topographic and thematic maps to report findings on spatial heterogeneity and variability of the objects and environmental processes on the Earth. The functionality of chosen GIS is in many cases tailored to the specific needs. For instance, some GIS are better suited for image processing, such as SAGA GIS \cite{CHALUPA2018174,Lemenkova2021a,Lemenkova2020a}, ENVI GIS, Erdas Imagine \cite{IWAHASHI2010202,AHN2010284}, Idrisi GIS \cite{AYALEW200515}, ILWIS GIS \cite{BABIKER2005127}, while others are best at vector data analysis and visualization, such as ArcGIS \cite{OHTA2022108201,IKEMI2017115,IWAHASHI201229,Klauco2013,NAKAYAMA2023110243,HANAOKA2014274}.

Providing a standardised solution of which GIS to select might be problematic, since in most cases GIS have a functionality of both raster and vector data analysis. In any case, whichever GIS is chosen, it requires active work in a menu of GUI with manipulation of input datasets and geospatial modelling to present cartographic visualization. Another problematic point in the selection of GIS software is its open source availability. The increasing variety of GIS necessitates that alternative more flexible and varied approaches to cartographic visualization and geospatial modelling should be explored. Such alternatives are presented by scripting approaches which can provide another functional tool for cartographic visualization and geospatial modelling.  Examples of scripting toolsets in cartography include GRASS GIS \cite{Lemenkova202016,Lemenkova202026} and GMT widely used in topographic and geomorphological mapping \cite{ijgi11090473,geosciences12030140}. 

Leaving apart the functionality of both tools as already widely discussed, this paper present the application of R language \cite{RCoreTeam} for spatial analysis and mapping. The main advantages of R consist in its open source access, straightforward language syntax, a scripting approach, a powerful functionality of its packages (libraries) which processes both tabular and geospatial data directly from a console. This enables R to be applied in various studies on geospatial data processing and visualisation using special packages, e.g., gstat \cite{HENGL20081886}, RStoolbox \cite{app122412554}, terra or raster \cite{jimaging8120317}.

The structure of this document is as follows. The research first discusses key issues relevant to geomorphometric modelling based on DEM in cartographic analysis of the geospatial data. Specifically, slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation are reviewed as important elements of the terrain analysis. Then study area of the Japanese archipelago is presented as a case example for mapping. It is then followed by a methodological description of the performed geomorphometric analysis using R and GMT. The methodological part demonstrates the screenshots of scripting in RStudio environment with maps of slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation, and GMT for 3D modelling. The results present the computed and visualized maps of the Japanese archipelago made in the RStudio environment. These include the geomorphometric elements (slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation) with statistical elements of data distribution as histograms, and a map of prefectures of Japan visualized by the ‘ggplot2’ graphical package of R. The discussion of the machine learning approach to cartographic modelling and visualization follows the results section. Finally, there is a discussion of advantages of using R in cartography instead of GIS and ways of modelling geospatial data by scripting languages.

\section{Materials and Methods}
The key used packages of R include ‘tmap’ for thematic mapping, ‘ggplot2’, ‘ggmap’ \cite{KahleWickham}, ‘maps’ and ‘mapdata’ for data capture and thematic regional mapping of prefectures of Japan. The package ‘mapdata’ contains the base location of the binary files of prefectures boundaries of Japan used by the map drawing functions. The ‘raster’ package was used for geomorphometric analysis which also included the depending packages, such as ‘sp’ (a package which operates with classes and methods for spatial data) and ‘sf’ (a package operating with ‘Simple Features’ as object classes in an R syntax). 

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}

The geodata processing in RStudio environment included following general scheme (Figure \ref{fig1}): 1) loading packages, 2) getting data, 3) inspecting data.frame, 4) set up coordinate system, 5) calculating terrain characteristics (slope, aspect, hillshade) by ‘raster’ package, 6) visualizing maps on a screen in RStudio, 7) plotting cartographic aesthetics in ‘tmap’ package, 8) mapping prefectures of Japan in ‘ggplot2’ package.

\unskip

The data for the terrain analysis includes digital elevation models (DEM) from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM). The SRTM is widely used in research \cite{RABUS2003241} due to its acceptable resolution (90m*90m) and open availability: the NASA Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) provided DEMs for over 80\% of the land surface on the Earth \cite{Ehsani}. The slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation maps were plotted using RStudio environment \cite{RStudio} using ‘raster’ package implemented by \cite{Hijmans}. Specifically the following workflow was used as pre-processing.

The data were captured using the ‘getData()’ function of the ‘raster’ package of R from the available geospatial datasets of the University of California, Davis campus. The data were then reprojected to the Lambert Conformal Conic projection (LCC) by the following function: $crs(alt) <- "+proj=lcc +lat\_1=-30 +lat\_2=40 +lon\_0=140 +datum=WGS84".$ The coordinate system is an important aspect of geographic data which is implemented in R environment by PROJ library \cite{EversKnudsen,Evenden1995,Evenden2005}.  

It should be specially mentioned that the SRTM shows elevation data shows not a bare-earth model but as surface, which includes dense canopy forests and built-up areas in the estimation of the terrain. Although such nuances might be worrying, in hydrological modelling, the SRTM is generally acceptable in country-level mapping of Japan based on DEM, as shown in this research.

\subsection{GMT scripting}
Scripting by Generic Mapping Tools (GMT) followed the existing experience in cartographic plotting \cite{su142315966} with a use of 3D packages for terrain modelling.

\subsection{Mapping prefectures of Japan}

This research used an experimental area of Japan to perform thematic regional mapping by R. The data were captured by the ‘maps’ package of R \cite{Beckeretal2013} and ‘mapdata’ which provide the map databases including the data on Japan: its prefectures, areas, etc. The novelty of this approach consists in using the ‘ggplot2’ package of R, a common graphical package for scientific visualization, specifically for cartographic purposes. Here the main elements (polygons of the Japan) were plotted using the ‘geom\_polygon’ function, which operates with data frames containing the coordinates of polygons and values associated with each of them: 
\begin{verbatim}geom_polygon(data=japan, aes(x=long, y=lat, fill=region, group=group)\end{verbatim} 

The annotations of the axes were added using ‘xlab’ and ‘ylab’ functions. The color scales were defined in the RColorBrewer, and extended from the default, fixed number of colors to the number of prefectures in Japan which was inspected by ‘length’ function as follows: 
\begin{verbatim}length(unique(japan$region))\end{verbatim} 
Afterwards, the number of colors in the color palette was defined using the ‘colourCount’ function: 
\begin{verbatim}colourCount = length(unique(japan$region))\end{verbatim}

Following that, the color palette was expanded to the required number (47 prefectures) by the use of ‘colorRampPalette’ function as follows: $colorRampPalette(brewer.pal(name="Spectral", n = 8))(47)$. 
Now when the color palette was adjusted to the dataset, the map of Japanese prefectures was colored by assigning each individual color to each of the 47 prefectures as follows: $scale\_fill\_manual(values = getPalette(colourCount))$. Other, additional cartographic elements included adding the tittles, subtitles and captions by ‘labs’ function which enables to modify labels, annotations and captions. The rest of the aesthetics has been added using theme() function as can be seen in Figure \ref{fig2} (left: script, right: resulting map). These include, among others, plotting the legend, its orientation and annotations, defining the text size and font characteristics, color of background, types of grid ticks, frequency of breaks on a cartographic grid. The final map is shown in Figure \ref{fig2}.

The map of prefectures (Figure \ref{fig2}) consisted in visualization of the 47 prefectures of Japan using ‘ggplot2’ package of R, as well as ‘mapdata’ and ‘maps’ packages used for data capture. Each prefectures was colored by the single-colored polygons modified from the default palette of R (‘Spectral’) using palette extension by the ‘colorRampPalette’ function. 

\subsection{Mapping geomorphological characteristics}
Modeling slope, aspect and hillshade is a technique for visualising terrain determined by DEM as numerical data source. Here the slope and aspect have been plotted first, following by a hillshade which is a derivative from the slope and aspect of the elevation and can be visually changed using various illuminating position of the light source (degree of angle rotation). The four derivative of the SRTM90 DEM were modeled and visualized: slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation grid using available methodology [25]. The slope gradient, aspect and hillshade were defined by the algorithm of the ‘raster’ package at any point of the input raster grid (SRTM90) using local neighbourhood analysis. These variables are automatically defined by the machine using values of altitude (elevation) and its derivatives at or around cell point on a raster grid representing an Earth land surface. The ‘alt’ has a formal class 'RasterLayer' of package ‘raster’ with 12 slots.

\paragraph{Slope}
The terrain characteristics of slope has been calculated and modeled using ‘raster’ package of R using following chain of commands. First the color palette of R was created for visualization: cols <- rainbow(255). Then, the slope and aspect have been computed by following: slope = terrain(alt, opt = "slope"). Afterwards, the slope:  plot(slope, col=cols, main='Slope', xlab = "lon", ylab = "lat"). Here ‘alt’ represent the abbreviation of ‘altitude’.

\paragraph{Aspect}
 The aspect has been modeled using function ‘terrain’ with option ‘aspect’ as follows: aspect = terrain(alt, opt = "aspect"), followed by the definition of colors: cols <- terrain.colors(255). Afterwards, using the same function plot() as for the slope modeling, the map of aspect has been plotted using the following code: plot(aspect, col=cols, main='Exposure', xlab = "lon", ylab = "lat").
 
 \paragraph{Hillshade} 
 The topographic hillshade represent the monochrome 3D view of the terrain relief, with the relative position of artificial illumination representing natural sun. It the attempts to visualize the relief using shading effects of the monochrome palette have been made since the onset of the GIS development [26–28]. The computing of hillshade has been done using the following algorithm: hill = hillShade(slope, aspect, angle = 40, direction = 270). The color palette has been applied using the code: cols <- topo.colors(255). Plotting the map has been done using plot function described above.
 
 \paragraph{Cartographic processing}
Mapping the geomorphological parameters in ‘tmap’ package \cite{JSSv084i06} included the processing of the computed raster layers by a specifically designed cartographic package where more elements could be added on a layout and more control on their aesthetics is available. Thus, the package is using the functions $tmap\_style$ where the general style of the layout was defined, $tm\_shape$ where the main data were given (name of the raster, title and a subtitle), $tm\_raster$ function where the method to process the color scale of a numeric variable (values of slopes in degrees) was set up and labels, and details on legend.  The $tm\_scale\_bar$ and $tm\_compass$ functions show the parameters of the annotations of the auxiliary elements on the map. The $tm\_layout$ function shows the variety of the cartographic aesthetics: fonts and ticks, color of panel labels, etc. 

The elements of map compose the whole aesthetics of the cartographic layout which is crucial for the overall visual appreciation by the reader. The map was then visualized on a screen in an RStudio environment by calling the ready raster (in this case, ‘map1’) for inspection and saved using the $tmap\_save$ function: $tmap\_save(map1, "Slope\_Japan.jpg", height = 7)$. The same procedure has been repeated with al the three files (slope, aspect, hillshade). The script visualized in an RStudio environment is presented in Figures in the Results section where left parts demonstrates the script and the right part shows the output maps, respectively.

\section{Results}

The results of the geospatial model of Japan are structured into regional mapping (prefectures) and geomorphological mapping (slope, aspect and hillshade sections), with various R packages used in the workflow, and with functional options related to visualization by RStudio discussed in previous sections. The presented maps based on SRTM90 DEM shown the geomorphometric models of slope, aspect and hillshade relief in Japan. 

The number of pixels (over 16,000 on a raster grid) was the greatest in the ‘gentle’ slope level (yellow color) comparing to the others: 12,000 pixels for the ‘moderate’ (orange color), over 10,000 for the ‘strong’ slope (light red color), 8,000 for ‘very strong’ (magenta color), three bins are covered by the class ‘extreme’ slope (purple color) and the rest (blue color) is represented by the less than 3,000 pixels (that is the steepest mountains slopes). 

The slope gradient, being a derivative of altitude presents a scale-dependent variables which changes in reduced or increased DEM resolution. Since the input data of this research is a 90*m SRTM DEM, the results of the slope modeling refer to the given resolution. The comparison of the exposure (Figure \ref{fig3}, left) and elevation maps (Figure \ref{fig3}, right) shows trends in the North-South-West-East direction with regard to the topographic altitude.

\section{Discussion}

Geomorphology is an important characteristics of the Earth surface reflected in landscape structure since it is associated with numerous environmental factors, such as vegetation coverage, habitats, hydrology, soil distribution, local micro-climate setting that are dependent on the topographic exposure, slope steepness and curvature of the relief and other spatial characteristics. In particular, geomorphological studies can be applied to environmental management and hydrological modelling (coastal retreat and fluvial processes), natural flood management [30] and presented as visualization of the correlation between the geomorphology, geophysical and physical geographic factors [31]. Various publications focused on Japan were published in particular, aimed to understand how the geomorphology of the archipelago, the geologic and surface processes, rock glacial topography are linked in the high mountain region of the Japanese Alps and also reflected in the submarine relief of the adjacent trench \cite{1974607,1980319,Lemenkova202012,Kariya2002}. 

Using new ways of measuring, sensing, and analyzing mountain geomorphology, It was noted \cite{SLAYMAKER2018230} that the uniqueness of mountain relief is reinforced by high elevation landforms and hydroclimate settings. This is supported by findings of \cite{Aoyama2005} on strong correlation between the geomorphology and environmental settings in Japan, where he reported that geomorphological and climate conditions during the Late Glacial led to a change in the environment in the northern Japanese Alps from a glacial to a permafrost environment in the current alpine zone. Other important findings in geomorphic studies in Japan are well summarized by \cite{OGUCHI20013} who noted extensive sedimentation in mountain piedmonts and coastal fluvial plains and abundant sediment in steep watersheds. They furthermore pointed at risk of hazardous hydro-geomorphological events in the areas of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions, as well as post glacial development of hill slopes and alluvial fans strongly affected by paleoclimate conditions. Besides the environmental factors, relief directly influences the social-economic patterns through the possibility of roads and buildings constructions and potential hazards of landslides which depend on the rock properties and slope steepness. 

The geomorphic processes are largely driven by Earth's gravity, which is partially controlled by the slope steepness of the relief. As a result, the intensity of the geomorphic processes, such as landslides,  is affected by displacement gradients connected to the geometric curvature of the surface. Thus, quantified slope steepness enables to calculate the rates at which landslides are stripping mountain slopes, which can be used for risk management and estimation of hazard risk caused by landslides \cite{FUJISAWA2010150,Chung}, in addition to climatic factors, such as precipitation intensity and repeatability of rainfall or downpours \cite{Tsunetaka,Oku} or seismic triggers, such as earthquakes or typhoons \cite{Yamada,Wangetal2010,HIRATA2019105217}. 

As a consequence, there is abundant research into topographic and bathymetric mapping, land surface classification based on DEM, relief and geomorphological modelling using various GIS, geomorphometric computations of slope and aspect, visualizing hillshade and DEM \cite{PRIMA2006373,LIN2004159,KASAI200957,GOMEZ201511,FisherTate,HAYAKAWA200690}. Since the geomorphometric studies should always be accompanies by a spatial topographic representation, the question of technical tools of mapping is always actual for the DEM based studies. This research demonstrated that the use of R in geomorphometric studies is effective and promising both for thematic maps plotting and for the spatial analysis.

\section{Conclusions}

Graphics and maps, done well, can provide an eye-catching and detailed information on the Earth’s surface. That is why the graphical approaches and methodology of visualization are of high importance in geographical sciences. Although having a specific language syntax and approaches to data analysis, R language is a very promising tool for geospatial visualization, geomorphological modelling and mapping Besides the statistical analysis of R applied in various research [46, 47], it can also be effectively used in mapping, as demonstrated in this study. Cartographic data visualization, whatever GIS software are used, is an integral part of the complex chain of the geospatial analysis that includes data capture, preprocessing, projecting, analysis of content of the datasets, modelling variables and soon. Applying machine learning methods to cartographic workflow facilitates the process of spatial analysis, helps increasing the accuracy and precision of the final results, and significantly decreases the time of data processing. The use of R in geographic analysis, however, is not yet as popular as traditional GIS, which can be explained by the non-trivial approach of using it in mapping and certain skills in coding.  

Topographic and geomorphological mapping, however, should aim at all research stages, at the machine-learning approach, which is less error-prone and time-consuming comparing to the traditional GIS. At the very least this can be the use of scripting for data visualization, like in GMT [48]. Better still, however, is using a full functionality of the programming [49–50],  markup and scripting languages [51–52]. Ideally, an advanced spatial analysis includes automatization of the cartographic processes through scripting which enables repeatability. It is through scripting that mapping is facilitated and fastened which, in turn, leads to the increased precision and accuracy. Cartographic layout is usually seen as the final stage of spatial analysis summarizing and presenting the results in a graphical form. That said, scripting by R largely facilitates graphic visualization, since similar parts of the script may be re-used for other maps in the same projection and spatial extent. Using R in cartographic workflow provided a route to the machine-learning methods in geodata analysis. In so doing, the emphasis towards creating a script-based data visualization offers both the increased precision of the geomorphological analysis, and provides an important advances in cartographic development.

In conclusion, the findings indicate that using R for mapping is another effective tool for spatial analysis besides GIS. It furthermore identified the issues, such as the need to use a variety of packages in R to fit in various tasks: geomorphometry, classic mapping, spatial analysis, data capture, data conversion are all were made using different packages. On the one hand it can be an easier than in GIS, but on the other hand, it can be a challenge since it require finding a suitable package, installing and activating it and using by applying its specific functions using syntax of R. Since the onset of the GIS development, technical methods of cartographic visualization, statistical plotting of data analysis in geosciences are being constantly developing [53–57]. This study makes a technical contribution to such development by adapting R language for mapping instead of traditional GIS (Klaučo et al. 2017). This paper shown application of R language for cartographic data visualization and geomorphometric analysis which included following general steps: 1) importing a SRTM90 DEM covering Japan; 2) visualizing a DEM using 'raster' package; 3) compute topographic surface parameters of slope, aspect and hillshade using R scripts demonstrated in the left parts of Figures \ref{fig4}, \ref{fig5} and \ref{fig6}, for repeatability; 4) cartographic visualization by 'tmap' package where the additional embellishments and cartographic elements (legend, histogram, grid, ticks, annotations, compass directions and a bar scale) were added on the plot.
