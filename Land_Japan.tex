% Abstract (Do not insert blank lines, i.e. \\) 
\abstract{In this paper, we describe the two related scripting methods of cartographic data processing and visualization that provide 2D and 3D mapping of Japan with different algorithm complexity. The first algorithm utilizes Generic Mapping Toolset (GMT) known as an advanced console-based program for spatial data processing. The modules of GMT combine the functionality of scripting with the aspects of geoinformatics which is especially effective for rapid analysis of large geospatial datasets, multi-format data processing and mapping in 2D and 3D modes. The second algorithm presents the use of the R programming language for cartographic visualization and spatial analysis. This R method utilizes the packages ‘tmap’, ‘raster’, ‘maps’, ‘mapdata’ to model the geomorphometric elements of the Japanese archipelago, such as slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation. The general purpose graphical package ‘ggplot2’ of R was used for mapping prefectures of Japan. The two scripting approaches demonstrated an established correspondence between the programming languages and cartography, which is determined with the use of scripts for data processing. They outperform several well-known and state-of-the-art GIS methods for mapping due to high automation of data processing. Cartography has largely reflected recent advances in data science, rapid development of scripting languages and transfer in the approaches of data processing. This extends to the shift from the traditional GIS to programming languages. As a response to these new challenges, we demonstrated in this paper the advantages of using scripts in mapping, which consist in repeatability and flexible applicability of scripts in similar works. The graphical plotting in 2D and 3D modes is performed in RStudio and GMT.}

\section{Introduction}

Topographic analysis based on DEM is considered one of the most important issues of cartography often used as a background information in geosciences. An inherent research step in the computations of the terrain analysis based on DEM is the use of modelling methods where the GIS is traditionally applied. A variety of existing GIS software can be applied to spatial data analysis which includes both socio-geographic and physical-geographic mapping. Modelling geospatial datasets by GIS aims at qualitative data analysis, cartographic visualization and interpretation of the topographic and thematic maps to report findings on spatial heterogeneity and variability of the objects and environmental processes on the Earth. The functionality of chosen GIS is in many cases tailored to the specific needs. For instance, some GIS are better suited for image processing, such as SAGA GIS [1–4], ENVI GIS, Erdas Imagine, Idrisi GIS [5], ILWIS GIS [6], while others are best at vector data analysis and visualization, such as ArcGIS [7–11].

Providing a standardized solution of which GIS to select might be problematic, since in most cases GIS have a functionality of both raster and vector data analysis. In any case, whichever GIS is chosen, it requires active work in a menu of GUI with manipulation of input datasets and geospatial modelling to present cartographic visualization. Another problematic point in the selection of GIS software is its open source availability. The increasing variety of GIS necessitates that alternative more flexible and varied approaches to cartographic visualization and geospatial modelling should be explored. Such alternatives are presented by scripting approaches which can provide another functional tool for cartographic visualization and geospatial modelling.  Examples of scripting toolsets in cartography include GRASS GIS [12] and GMT widely used in topographic and geomorphological mapping [13–14]. Leaving apart the functionality of both tools as already widely discussed, this paper present the application of R language [15] for spatial analysis and mapping. The main advantages of R consists in its open source access, straightforward language syntax, a scripting approach, a powerful functionality of its packages (libraries) which processes both tabular and geospatial data directly from a console. 

The structure of this document is as follows. The research first discusses key issues relevant to geomorphometric modelling based on DEM in cartographic analysis of the geospatial data. Specifically, slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation are reviewed as important elements of the terrain analysis. Then study area of the Japanese archipelago is presented as a case example for mapping. It is then followed by a methodological description of the performed geomorphometric analysis using R.

The methodological part demonstrates the screenshots of scripting in RStudio environment and resulting maps of slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation.  The key used packages of R include ‘tmap’ for thematic mapping, ‘ggplot2’, ‘ggmap’ [16], ‘maps’ and ‘mapdata’ for data capture and thematic regional mapping of prefectures of Japan. The package ‘mapdata’ contains the base location of the binary files of prefectures boundaries of Japan used by the map drawing functions. The ‘raster’ package was used for geomorphometric analysis which also included the depending packages, such as ‘sp’ (a package which operates with classes and methods for spatial data) and ‘sf’ (a package operating with ‘Simple Features’ as object classes in an R syntax). The results present the computed and visualized maps of the Japanese archipelago made in the RStudio environment. These include the geomorphometric elements (slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation) with statistical elements of data distribution as histograms, and a map of prefectures of Japan visualized by the ‘ggplot2’ graphical package of R. The discussion of the machine learning approach to cartographic modelling and visualization follows the results section. Finally, there is a discussion of advantages of using R in cartography instead of GIS and ways of modelling geospatial data by scripting languages.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}

The geodata processing in RStudio environment included following general scheme (Figure 1): 1) loading packages, 2) getting data, 3) inspecting data.frame, 4) set up coordinate system, 5) calculating terrain characteristics (slope, aspect, hillshade) by ‘raster’ package, 6) visualizing maps on a screen in RStudio, 7) plotting cartographic aesthetics in ‘tmap’ package, 8) mapping prefectures of Japan in ‘ggplot2’ package. 

The data for the terrain analysis includes digital elevation models (DEM) from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM). The SRTM is widely used in research [17] due to its acceptable resolution (90m*90m) and open availability: the NASA Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) provided DEMs for over 80\% of the land surface on the Earth [18]. The slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation maps were plotted using RStudio environment [19] using ‘raster’ package implemented by [20]. Specifically the following workflow was used as pre-processing.

The data were captured using the ‘getData()’ function of the ‘raster’ package of R from the available geospatial datasets of the University of California, Davis campus. The data were then reprojected to the Lambert Conformal Conic projection (LCC) by the following function: $crs(alt) <- "+proj=lcc +lat\_1=-30 +lat\_2=40 +lon\_0=140 +datum=WGS84".$ The coordinate system is an important aspect of geographic data which is implemented in R environment by PROJ library [21–23].  

It should be specially mentioned that the SRTM shows elevation data shows not a bare-earth model but as surface, which includes dense canopy forests and built-up areas in the estimation of the terrain. Although such nuances might be worrying, in hydrological modelling, the SRTM is generally acceptable in country-level mapping of Japan based on DEM, as shown in this research.

\subsection{Mapping prefectures of Japan}

This research used an experimental area of Japan to perform thematic regional mapping by R. The data were captured by the ‘maps’ package of R [24] and ‘mapdata’ which provide the map databases including the data on Japan: its prefectures, areas etc. The novelty of this approach consists in using the ‘ggplot2’ package of R, a common graphical package for scientific visualization, specifically for cartographic purposes. Here the main elements (polygons of the Japan) were plotted using the ‘geom\_polygon’ function, which operates with data frames containing the coordinates of polygons and values associated with each of them: 
\begin{verbatim}geom_polygon(data=japan, aes(x=long, y=lat, fill=region, group=group)\end{verbatim} 

The annotations of the axes were added using ‘xlab’ and ‘ylab’ functions. The color scales were defined in the RColorBrewer, and extended from the default, fixed number of colors to the number of prefectures in Japan which was inspected by ‘length’ function as follows: 
\begin{verbatim}length(unique(japan$region))\end{verbatim} 
Afterwards, the number of colors in the color palette was defined using the ‘colourCount’ function: 
\begin{verbatim}colourCount = length(unique(japan$region))\end{verbatim}

Following that, the color palette was expanded to the required number (47 prefectures) by the use of ‘colorRampPalette’ function as follows: $colorRampPalette(brewer.pal(name="Spectral", n = 8))(47)$. 
Now when the color palette was adjusted to the dataset, the map of Japanese prefectures was colored by assigning each individual color to each of the 47 prefectures as follows: $scale\_fill\_manual(values = getPalette(colourCount))$. Other, additional cartographic elements included adding the tittles, subtitles and captions by ‘labs’ function which enables to modify labels, annotations and captions. The rest of the aesthetics has been added using theme() function as can be seen in Fig. 2 (left: script, right: resulting map). These include, among others, plotting the legend, its orientation and annotations, defining the text size and font characteristics, color of background, types of grid ticks, frequency of breaks on a cartographic grid. The final map is shown in Fig. 2. 

\subsection{Mapping geomorphological characteristics}
Modeling slope, aspect and hillshade is a technique for visualising terrain determined by DEM as numerical data source. Here the slope and aspect have been plotted first, following by a hillshade which is a derivative from the slope and aspect of the elevation and can be visually changed using various illuminating position of the light source (degree of angle rotation). The four derivative of the SRTM90 DEM were modeled and visualized: slope, aspect, hillshade and elevation grid using available methodology [25]. The slope gradient, aspect and hillshade were defined by the algorithm of the ‘raster’ package at any point of the input raster grid (SRTM90) using local neighbourhood analysis. These variables are automatically defined by the machine using values of altitude (elevation) and its derivatives at or around cell point on a raster grid representing an Earth land surface. The ‘alt’ has a formal class 'RasterLayer' of package ‘raster’ with 12 slots.

\paragraph{Slope}
The terrain characteristics of slope has been calculated and modeled using ‘raster’ package of R using following chain of commands. First the color palette of R was created for visualization: cols <- rainbow(255). Then, the slope and aspect have been computed by following: slope = terrain(alt, opt = "slope"). Afterwards, the slope:  plot(slope, col=cols, main='Slope', xlab = "lon", ylab = "lat"). Here ‘alt’ represent the abbreviation of ‘altitude’.

\paragraph{Aspect}
 The aspect has been modeled using function ‘terrain’ with option ‘aspect’ as follows: aspect = terrain(alt, opt = "aspect"), followed by the definition of colors: cols <- terrain.colors(255). Afterwards, using the same function plot() as for the slope modeling, the map of aspect has been plotted using the following code: plot(aspect, col=cols, main='Exposure', xlab = "lon", ylab = "lat").
 
 \paragraph{Hillshade} 
 The topographic hillshade represent the monochrome 3D view of the terrain relief, with the relative position of artificial illumination representing natural sun. It the attempts to visualize the relief using shading effects of the monochrome palette have been made since the onset of the GIS development [26–28]. The computing of hillshade has been done using the following algorithm: hill = hillShade(slope, aspect, angle = 40, direction = 270). The color palette has been applied using the code: cols <- topo.colors(255). Plotting the map has been done using plot function described above.
 
 \paragraph{Cartographic processing}
Mapping the geomorphological parameters in ‘tmap’ package [29] included the processing of the computed raster layers by a specifically designed cartographic package where more elements could be added on a layout and more control on their aesthetics is available. Thus, the package is using the functions $tmap\_style$ where the general style of the layout was defined, $tm\_shape$ where the main data were given (name of the raster, title and a subtitle), $tm\_raster$ function where the method to process the color scale of a numeric variable (values of slopes in degrees) was set up and labels, and details on legend.  The $tm\_scale\_bar$ and $tm\_compass$ functions show the parameters of the annotations of the auxiliary elements on the map. The $tm\_layout$ function shows the variety of the cartographic aesthetics: fonts and ticks, color of panel labels, etc. 

The elements of map compose the whole aesthetics of the cartographic layout which is crucial for the overall visual appreciation by the reader. The map was then visualized on a screen in an RStudio environment by calling the ready raster (in this case, ‘map1’) for inspection and saved using the $tmap\_save$ function: $tmap\_save(map1, "Slope\_Japan.jpg", height = 7)$. The same procedure has been repeated with al the three files (slope, aspect, hillshade). The script visualized in an RStudio environment is presented in Fig. 4, Fig. 5, and Fig. 6. where the left parts shows the script and the right part shows the output maps, respectively.

\section{Results}

The results of the geospatial model of Japan are structured into regional mapping (prefectures) and geomorphological mapping (slope, aspect and hillshade sections), with various R packages used in the workflow, and with functional options related to visualization by RStudio discussed in previous sections. The presented maps based on SRTM90 DEM shown the geomorphometric models of slope, aspect and hillshade relief in Japan. The slope map shows the steepness of the mountains, highs and hills in the raster grids in different sub-regions of Japan. The slope directions (Figure 4) revealed following variations in the data: ‘gentle’, ‘moderate’, ‘strong’, ‘very strong’, ‘extreme’ and ‘steep’ slopes of the mountainous regions of Japan. 

The number of pixels (over 16,000 on a raster grid) was the greatest in the ‘gentle’ slope level (yellow color) comparing to the others: 12,000 pixels for the ‘moderate’ (orange color), over 10,000 for the ‘strong’ slope (light red color), 8,000 for ‘very strong’ (magenta color), three bins are covered by the class ‘extreme’ slope (purple color) and the rest (blue color) is represented by the less than 3,000 pixels (that is the steepest mountains slopes). The slope gradient, being a derivative of altitude presents a scale-dependent variables which changes in reduced or increased DEM resolution. Since the input data of this research is a 90*m SRTM DEM, the results of the slope modeling refer to the given resolution.

The comparison of the exposure (Figure 3, left) and elevation maps (Figure 3, right) shows trends in the North-South-West-East direction with regard to the topographic altitude. The aspect map (Figure 5) demonstrates that slope orientation by compass direction (W-E-S-N) differs in various parts of the mountains chains of the Japanese Alps. In central part of the area (around 36° N) many slopes have west orientation (red color) which is correlated to the geographic distribution of the Central Alps, but in other parts the southern (yellow color) and eastern (orange color) orientation are the prevailing values. Similarly the northern orientation (green color) of the slopes contributes the lest in the dataset.

Furthermore, the relationship of the slope aspect and elevation values (the altitude of the mountains) might be the points of correlation, which should be emphasized as the impact of the geomorphic patterns on the geomorphometric characteristics which helps, detecting trends showing relief in the Japanese Alps. The hillshade map (Figure 6) shows more complex modelling base don slope and aspect. The levels of illumination was set as at angle = 40, azimuthal direction = 270. The elevation map was used as a basis for the relief overlaid by the hill shading. The map of prefectures (Figure 2) consisted in visualization of the 47 prefectures of Japan using ‘ggplot2’ package of R, as well as ‘mapdata’ and ‘maps’ packages used for data capture. Each prefectures was colored by the single-colored polygons modified from the default palette of R (‘Spectral’) using palette extension by the ‘colorRampPalette’ function. 
	
The results of the SRTM90 DEM modelling identified geomorphometric main characteristics, such as slope, aspect and hillshade and shown an uneven elevation in the topographic map using R scripting. Using a variety of R packages implies both the data processing, exploring data frames, manipulation with its structure and spatial data analysis (e.g. using 'raster' package), and cartographic aesthetic visualization of the layouts supported by specially designed packages such as 'tmap', 'maps', 'mapdata'.
